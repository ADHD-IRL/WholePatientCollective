The gap

Modern care is locally optimized and globally blind.

A person with several conditions sees several providers, each working from a partial chart. Each decision can be sound on its own. The trouble lives in the seams — where no single clinician is looking.

  1. 1

    Diagnostic anchoring

    A second condition gets dismissed as a symptom of the first because nobody questions the original frame — fatigue blamed on depression when it's really an untreated thyroid or medication issue.

  2. 2

    Medication interference

    Prescriptions add up across silos. Each one is safe alone; together they cause interactions, duplication, or a "prescribing cascade" where one drug treats another's side effect.

  3. 3

    Treatment conflict

    Goals collide. A medicine that helps one condition can worsen another, and nobody weighs the trade-off for the whole person.

"This is a reasoning-under-fragmentation problem, not only a data problem. The data often exists — nobody synthesizes it."

What we believe

Four principles for care that sees the whole person.

Consumer-driven

You have a legal right to your records, and providers now have to hand them over through patient-facing APIs. The person at the center decides where data goes and what gets looked at.

Empowered

Walking into an appointment with good questions changes the conversation. Our tools help you bring those questions — not diagnoses, not instructions — to the clinicians who can act on them.

Augmented

AI reads the whole picture at once and argues with itself about what doesn't add up. People stay in charge: the system flags and explains its reasoning, and a licensed clinician makes the call.

Holistic

One person, not a list of organ systems. Medications, diagnoses, labs, and the everyday context only a family member knows, all brought together so trade-offs get weighed for the whole person.

How it works

Get the experts who never meet into one room, and let them argue.

Today's care is a panel of specialists who never talk to each other. We bring that panel together as AI agents, each one playing a specialty with its usual blind spots, plus a skeptic whose job is to ask what else could explain the findings. The places where they can't agree are where buried problems tend to be.

Front door

Caregiver & patient

Brings reach, consent, and lived context no record holds.

The engine

Unified model + debate

Normalizes records from ≥ 2 providers, runs deterministic checks, then adversarial reasoning.

Back door

Clinician

Brings judgment and the authority to act. Always decides.

New information re-runs the debate. The contested-findings list is where the two humans meet.

Ingest

Records pulled from wherever they live, through TEFCA's Individual Access Services or direct SMART on FHIR endpoints, after IAL2 identity proofing.

Normalize

Everything mapped to shared vocabularies (RxNorm, SNOMED CT, ICD-10-CM, LOINC), with a provenance trail on every fact.

Check

Deterministic checks catch the known problems cheaply: interactions, duplicate prescriptions, contraindications.

Debate

Specialty agents and a skeptic argue over what's left. The output is a ranked list of contested findings, each with its reasoning trace. It is never a diagnosis.

One finding, two renderings

Same reasoning. Different language for each person.

Both views come from the same structured finding. The caregiver sees a pattern and one question to ask. The clinician sees the suspected mechanism with every fact's source and date, including the one thing neither doctor could have known: they had no shared record.

A new medicine may be treating a side effect of another one

About three weeks after a blood pressure medicine was started, a different doctor prescribed a water pill for ankle swelling. Ankle swelling is a known side effect of some blood pressure medicines.

Question to bring

"Could the ankle swelling be related to the blood pressure medicine, and should both of my doctors know about each other's prescriptions?"

This is a question to discuss, not a conclusion. Don't start, stop, or change any medicine without your clinician.

Our first tool

Bring Better Questions

The first product we can ship is the caregiver side, simplified. You enter your own information by typing it or pasting from a patient portal or after-visit summary. The AI reads it all together and gives you informed questions to take to your clinician.

  1. Load what you have. Medicines, diagnoses, procedures, notes, from every provider.
  2. One read of the whole picture. Guardrails keep the output to questions, never conclusions.
  3. Walk in prepared. A question list you can copy, the findings behind it sorted by priority, and suggestions for what to add for a fuller read.
  • No health information is stored. Your request goes in, questions come out, and nothing is kept.
  • API keys live only on the server and never reach your browser.
  • Medication flags will be checked against a drug-interaction database.

Roadmap

Start small and useful, then build toward the full system.

The data plumbing is bought, not built, because the value is in the reasoning. The question the whole plan has to answer: does adversarial debate over an aggregated record catch issues that siloed care missed, with few enough false alarms that clinicians find it worth reading?

  1. Phase 0

    Foundations & guardrails

    Legal basis, clinical advisor, and a written success metric.

  2. Phase 1

    Unified patient model

    Aggregator API with IAL2, five resource types from at least two sources, provenance on every fact.

  3. Phase 2

    Deterministic checks

    Interaction, duplication, and contraindication checks, which also serve as ground truth.

  4. Phase 3

    The debate layer

    Specialty agents plus a skeptic, disagreement scoring, and hard safety rails. This is what sets us apart.

  5. Phase 4

    Two surfaces & pilot

    Caregiver and clinician front ends, scored on consented retrospective records.
